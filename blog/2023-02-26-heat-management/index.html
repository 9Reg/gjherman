Heat Management

The third article my three-part-series discussing near-future technologies for spacecraft. This time, heat management, an oft ignored topic in science fiction.

In my last article, I argued that steel would be the hull material of choice for most spacefaring vessels because of how and where those ships would be manufactured and what types of lives, those ships would have. (Spoiler: In microgravity on captured and orbited S- and M-type asteroids with vertically integrated mining-to-manufacturing factories.) In this article, I discuss spaceship heat management — a major issue for inner-solar spacecraft — , and how selecting steel as a primary hull material can exacerbate those heat-management issues.

As a hull material, steel has two primary deficiencies: It has a low thermal conductivity and a high coefficient of thermal expansion. Both issues are relevant in any discussion on heat management in aerospace applications, with the first being a measure of how fast a material absorbs or releases heat and the second measuring how much a material expands as it’s heated, gaining volume and losing density.

For most fictional worlds — especially in Science Fantasy — , hull materials and heat management are irrelevant or glossed over. I too don’t even mention it in my second book, which introduces the space-faring era of the Arcworld. But, as these articles are as much futurist entertainment with a bit of a gentle advertisement for the world I build, so too are they also a forum to fine tune that world and the science therein.

An Intro to Heat

Spaceships have to deal with a wide range of temperatures during their normal operations. For example, satellites in Low Earth Orbit (LEO; like the International Space Station) will experience temperatures that range from -65℃ to 125℃ (reference: warning, MS Word download). For deep-space applications, a spaceship will encounter temperatures just above absolute zero. But, except for deep-solar transports, I would argue that most ships would be much closer to the sun (and thereby experience temperatures similar to Earth’s LEO) for one simple reason: sunlight. Sunlight is required by humanity, especially the G2 frequency, and it’s a good source of electricity. We already use radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTG) in our spacecraft, but that doesn’t provide sufficient power for habitation, so a full-on fission reactor (or perhaps in the future fusion) would be required for deep-space craft, and that just raises the costs, increases the risks, and adds exponentially more heat that needs management. Therefore, I believe that most habitation would be within sunlight’s reach.

Let’s look at an example. Saturn, a planet with a magnetosphere that protects many of its moons, is a suitable location for human colonization, after Mars and the moon. This is because it has one moon which contains a methane/ethane fuel source (Titan) and another with abundant water (Enceladus). But, Saturn is 9.5 times as far away from the sun as is the Earth and therefore only receives [1.1% of the solar radiation that Earth encounters](https://web.archive.org/web/20230528185521/https://www.nasa.gov/audience/foreducators/9-12/features/F_How_Far_How_Faint.html ). This means that unless there’s unlimited fuel to burn (e.g., frequent dipping into Titan’s lakes), most spaceships (without fusion/fission reactors) will require solar panels in order to generate electricity, and with the sun’s intensity at 1.1% as it is on Earth, those solar panels in use around Saturn need to be super efficient or gigantically large for the same power output that would be found on Earth or in Earth’s orbit.

For this reason alone, space colonization will most likely be concentrated with a half AU (astronomical unit; the distance between the sun and the Earth) of Earth’s orbit, at least in the beginning. Mars, at 1.5 AU, receives only 44% of the solar radiation as the Earth, and Venus at 0.7 AU gets 90% more sunlight. With this data, it becomes easier to see how the colonization of space will be limited by a few hard facts. Perhaps solar panels placed nearer Venus’ orbit could beam that energy somewhere by microwave or laser, but more realistically, significant advances in solar panels (and bending the laws of photonics) would be required to make deep-space solar panels, and their usage, feasible.

Near-Earth Habitation

For reasons of simplicity, gravity, and resources, most near-Earth habitation will be centered on rocky planets (Mars), Earth’s moon, or captive asteroids (deceleration is a pain). Heat management on Mars will primarily focus on heat retention, as with a small atmosphere, conductive heat loss (besides radiative) will be a concern. Heat management on the moon will be even more critical than with a similarly placed spaceship, as the moon colony will be subject to the whims of the moon’s rotation around the Earth, meaning that half the time it will be in constant sunlight and the other half in constant darkness. Therefore, half the time the settlers will be worried about excess heat and the other half about freezing to death. In this instance, heat sinks will become relevant (more on that later).

As mentioned in the beginning, the International Space Station (ISS) has to deal with a very wide range of temperatures that can get up to 125°C. It collects that heat as it rotates around the earth, exposing its different sides to direct sunlight. Besides that, it also generates a lot of its own heat. Within a spaceship, everything electronic generates heat, especially the solar panels and their transmission lines. This heat has to go somewhere: either from the warm (sunlit) side to the cold (dark) side, or released into space. For this excess heat release, the ISS has large aluminum radiators. As there’s no (or practically no) atmosphere, conductive release is impossible, therefore only radiative release is feasible, which has a direct influence on the size of the radiators. Just check out the giant aluminum radiators on the [ISS](https://web.archive.org/web/20210515155146/https://science.nasa.gov/science-news/science-at-nasa/2001/ast21mar_1 ).

Why did they use aluminum? Because of aluminum’s thermal conductivity, which is several times higher than steel. There are better conductors, though. Both silver and copper are twice as good, but both are expensive and heavy for use on spaceships. Thus, aluminum is the most cost-effective option regarding weight (fuel requirements for launch), cost, and availability on deep-space asteroids (orbital manufacturing).

The ISS also has a super-complex heat-management system for those aforementioned extreme temperature fluctuations as it orbits the Earth sixteen times per day. To manage that heat, the ISS feeds its radiators with [liquid ammonia](https://web.archive.org/web/20210515155146/https://science.nasa.gov/science-news/science-at-nasa/2001/ast21mar_1 ), to which heat from liquid water has been transferred. This transfer is required, as water would freeze as soon as it left the confines of the hull and was sent to the radiators. It does this with relatively little liquid of both types.

How could this concept work on Mars or the moon? It could be coupled with the mining of water.

By sending excess heat to the mining heads in the ice during the two weeks of constant sunlight, when the surface [temperature increases to 120°C](https://web.archive.org/web/20220319102436/https://lunar.gsfc.nasa.gov/images/lithos/LROlitho7temperaturevariation27May2014.pdf ), that fluid would be used to melt as much ice as possible into an in situ pool at the face of the mine. This water would be electrolyzed into environmental oxygen and fuel, but if the system was large enough, then liquid water could also be used to store excess heat for re-use during the two weeks of total darkness when surface temperatures decline to -130°C (caverns, such as the Hermite Crater can get down to -250°C).

Using a heat sink in this manner wouldn’t be possible for orbital spaceships, but craft in space have a distinct advantage. They can rotate themselves to ensure that each side of the ship receives the same amount of sunlight. But that wouldn’t be possible in all situations, such as when docked. That, too, would require radiators and water/ammonia systems to eliminate the extra heat. But how much water would be required to ensure that heat is dissipated from the hotspots efficiently and rapidly? If we’re now using steel as a primary hull material, then it will retain heat and not transfer to water (or liquid ammonia) as efficiently as aluminum or copper would. Therefore, considerably more amounts of liquid would be required for this purpose.

But aerospace engineers have already answered the question of where to find that mass of liquid. Fuel tanks comprise most of an airplane that is designed for extended range, and this fuel can be used in place of water to transfer heat away from sensitive electronics (as with the F35) or engines (as is most likely with the B-21 Raider). This too can be expected design when dealing with spacecraft.

Therefore, near-earth habitation will use a combination of rotation, not just for gravity but also for heat management, use fuel as an additional heat-sink or heat-transfer medium in order to equalize the diverse temperature gradients a ship is prone to experience, and have massive aluminum radiators to expel excess heat. All of this will be reliant on an efficient and failure-free (multiple backups) heat transfer system of pumps and tubing.

Deep-Space Exploration / Habitation

Heat becomes less of an issue the farther from the sun, not because excess heat doesn’t build up, but because it’s easier to mitigate the buildup. Ships residing in 1% of the sun’s radiative heat will not experience drastic changes in area heat, meaning the sunny side will have a temperature close to the shadowed side — both close to absolute zero.

So, in deep-space, heat management will mostly be about retaining heat and distributing it equally, rather than only dispersing it into the vacuum of space. This means few windows, as they lose heat easily, and internal materials with low thermal conductivity. This is where steel can help with its ability to store heat well and disperse it equally. No matter how far a spaceship is away from the sun, the electronics and people will still generate significant amounts of heat, and keeping that inside will be of primary concern. Constant sunlight, no matter how slight, will still have an effect, even at those distances from the sun. Any material with lower thermal conductivity will help a more simplified thermal management system that moves heat around instead of sending it to radiators. For ships never destined to be within 3–5AU of the sun, there may even be no need for radiators. If there is, then they’d be significantly smaller and the thermal systems simplified.

Fusion and fission reactors will probably play a more prominent role in the design of ships destined for deep-space missions. They’ll probably have some of the same heat-management systems though, but rather than being designed for expelling heat, they’d be designed for spreading the heat that’s generated around by the reactors or engines equally.

Transports

Ships used for transport will have to operate in a much greater environmental range than those that are habitation-focused. They will have the advantage of being able to reposition themselves when in stasis, or maneuver themselves between sunlight and shadow when near celestial bodies, but they cannot do so under acceleration. With the vast distances between all the places that mankind will want to go in the solar system, spaceflight will still be an extremely calculated and controlled affair (as it is today). Using various gravity wells for slingshotting, fuel burns that are timed to the closest millisecond, and perfectly calculated engine orientations mean that rotating a ship for heat equalization while in flight will rarely be an option. Instead, fairly simple systems would need to be devised that used the heat differential between different liquids to draw heat away from that ship’s engines and into the fuel (as above with the F35) that would then subsequently be combusted out the engine, capturing, collecting and expelling that heat all in one process.

I didn’t make a differentiation between transports that would enter planetary or lunar gravity wells and those destined to live their entire lives in microgravity, but the concepts would be roughly the same, the primary difference being their hull materials and how much heat the hull holds.

I carry this concept further in my books where the dropship Vanir’s hull consists primarily of a woven carbon and ceramic, non-ablative, composite material. It pushes the limits of materials technology well beyond current practice, but it’s also hard science fiction, meaning I am allowed to make a few suppositions in its composition. But you’ll hear more about Vanir’s construction in Book Three of the Arcworld Saga soon enough. In that explanation, I rely heavily on my predictions above, plus throw in a few innovative advancements, especially in the realm of fluid dynamics and surface tension potential. In effect, the concept is the same. The massive fuel tanks pipe fuel through the carbon-ceramic composite skin to both heat and radiate, depending on need. But I’ll stop there and let you read Vanir and The Fall to learn more about my world.

Conclusion

Nothing has been introduced here that scientists with experience in spacecraft thermal control technologies don’t know, nor is anything far-fetched. The principles of thermodynamics exist not at our whim, even as science fiction authors. Rather, I used this opportunity to discuss the current state of science and how it could apply in a potential future. That is where the Arcworld exists, where suspension of belief isn’t requisite in any but a few instances. The science of the Arcworld should be fully plausible with respects to space travel because I don’t want to force the reader to accept too many implausible realities. I’m already proposing that wormholes can be controlled and used for travel. That’s enough fanciful belief suspension already.
